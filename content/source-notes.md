# Source notes

These notes preserve the complete argument structure of the eight study-guide documents used to build the site. They are document-level working notes, not a replacement for a formal bibliography. The numbered references embedded in the original documents are not reproduced as formal citations because the directory did not include the underlying bibliography.

## `NACA Human Computers.md` — the central claim

- Human computers were an essential but routinely undervalued part of NACA's research system. They were not isolated arithmetic aides: they were the computational engine connecting experiments to design decisions from early aviation through the Space Age.
- The recurring division of labor was: engineers framed tests, mechanics built models, instruments generated readings, human computers reduced and verified data, and engineers interpreted the resulting curves.
- The basic data path ran from wind-tunnel or flight readings, through transcription and calculation on multi-column sheets with Marchant or Friden desk calculators, through independent verification, to plotted lift, drag, and stability curves.
- Their work supported P-38 dive-recovery flaps, Richard Whitcomb's area rule, Vera Huckel's sonic-boom field studies, and trajectory/navigation work associated with Katherine Johnson and Dorothy Vaughan.
- The contradiction at the center of the history is structural: indispensable technical work was classified as low-status support work. Female computers were hired into lower-paid Sub-Professional grades, were often omitted from reports, and were trivialized in internal publications.
- Black women including Dorothy Vaughan, Mary Jackson, and Kathryn Peddrew faced the combined effects of gender hierarchy and racial segregation in West Area Computing.
- Electronic computers did not simply replace the women. Human computers wrote programs, wired plugboards, managed punch cards, authored operating guides, trained engineers, and verified machine output. The transition was gradual and often occurred through new technical roles or attrition.
- Beverly Golemba's recovery work shows how fragile this history is: personnel files were lost or destroyed, and only 13 surviving women could be located for interviews from a workforce of hundreds.

## `NACA Human Computers (1).md` — why the labor was needed

- NACA faced a severe data-processing bottleneck. Before computing pools, engineers often performed their own post-test arithmetic, slowing the next round of experiments and allowing unchecked mistakes to persist.
- A 1942 Langley memorandum described engineers doing routine calculation as “experience being wasted.” Trained human computers could complete in hours work that had consumed an engineer's day.
- New atmospheric, high-speed, transonic, and supersonic wind tunnels multiplied the quantity of numerical data. Manometer readings and photographic tapes had to be transcribed, reduced to dimensionless coefficients, plotted, and compared.
- The Second World War simultaneously increased the urgency of aircraft research and reduced the supply of male technical workers through enlistment and the draft.
- NACA recruited women with college training in mathematics and physics, many from secondary-school teaching. The agency gained mathematical expertise at lower Civil Service salary grades.
- The computing pools completed a four-part research ecosystem: engineers designed the test, mechanics built the model, human computers processed and plotted the results, and engineers used those results to change the design.
- Reliability came from peer verification. A second computer recalculated completed work; disagreements were marked with a red dot and returned for correction.

## `NACA Human Computing.md` — the knowledge-production workflow

- The workflow was a closed feedback loop and the “computational nervous system” of experimental aeronautics.
- Eight stages recur: formulate the question and build the model; run the model or aircraft; capture pressure/flow readings; transcribe raw values; apply formulas and constants; plot curves and cross-plots; independently verify; interpret and revise the next test.
- Instruments and representations included liquid-filled manometer boards, photographic tapes, Schlieren images, slide rules, pre-printed computing sheets, Marchant and Friden calculators, graph paper, and metal-arm integrators.
- Computers produced non-dimensional aerodynamic coefficients and graphs such as lift versus angle of attack at several Mach numbers.
- Error had material consequences for pilot safety and national defense. Heat and humidity could smudge sheets; dark reading rooms and tapes caused eye strain; repetitive calculator work could injure wrists.
- The red-dot system required a second computer to recalculate and sign the work. Long assignment to a particular tunnel also created domain knowledge: computers knew what plausible curves looked like and flagged anomalies or bad inputs.
- Human computers improved the workflow itself. Emma Jean Landrum designed a ruler-gauge for reading manometer tapes, while Helen Willey helped standardize work through a data-reduction handbook.
- This pipeline made data usable in work on the area rule, slotted-throat tunnels, dive-recovery flaps, sonic-boom signatures, and re-entry studies.

## `NACA Human Computers (2).md` — recruitment and working conditions

- Wartime emergency changed NACA's hiring. National bulletins targeted women graduates in mathematics and physics, many of whom had expected to teach.
- Women entered as SP-1/Junior Computers at roughly $1,400–$1,620 annually. The economic rationale was explicit: their labor preserved engineers' time at lower cost.
- Elton W. Miller used a two-week screening workshop to overcome internal resistance. Candidates were evaluated for mathematics as well as “attitude, appearance, and willingness to work at night,” revealing the gendered terms of entry.
- Workspaces were improvised in cubbyholes, glass-enclosed model-shop balconies, porches, and noisy shop floors. Before air conditioning, summer heat caused fainting and perspiration threatened paper records.
- Manometer and tape reading produced eye strain; continuous use of heavy desk calculators caused repetitive injury. Emma Jean Landrum moved to a left-handed desk after wrist damage.
- Pools worked double shifts, six days a week, including nights, Sundays, and holidays. Wind tunnels and calculating machines sometimes operated when municipal electrical demand was lower.
- Custom desks held shoe-box-sized calculators in left- or right-hand wells so the operator could transcribe and key figures together.
- Hampton's housing shortage placed single women in Anne Wythe Hall or private rooms. Gas rationing and limited late transit produced “riding combinations,” shared carpools that sometimes crossed occupational rank and gender.
- Black recruits encountered segregated housing and were assigned to West Area Computing with separate dining and restroom facilities. When loaned to other units, West Area staff were often given the least desirable repetitive datasets.
- The distinction between Computer, Mathematician, and Engineer institutionalized lower status. Helen Willey had to lobby for qualified women to receive Mathematician ratings.
- Internal culture could reduce technical workers to appearance: *Air Scoop* described “cute youngsters in bobby socks,” while satire acknowledged that engineers received praise and computers absorbed blame.

## `Hidden Computers.md` — gender, race, and professional status

- Gender, race, and job classification produced the central contradiction: essential mathematical work was structurally subordinate.
- Women with mathematics and physics degrees received Sub-Professional or Junior Computer classifications while men with comparable bachelor's degrees entered Professional engineering grades.
- Lower salary, the routine use of “girls,” appearance-focused publicity, omission from reports, and uneven access to promotion all diminished professional status.
- Daily cooperation with engineers could be respectful while the institution remained unequal. Advancement often depended on a supervisor willing to advocate for an individual woman.
- Virginia segregation shaped Langley. Kathryn Peddrew, Dorothy Vaughan, Mary Jackson, and other Black women entered West Area Computing, used segregated facilities, and navigated both explicit and unwritten boundaries after formal integration began.
- Dorothy Vaughan arrived in 1943, became NACA's first African American supervisor in 1958, taught herself FORTRAN, and prepared her staff for programming work including Scout launch-vehicle research.
- Mary Jackson entered in 1951, joined the otherwise white 4-Foot Supersonic Tunnel group, defended correct calculations when given bad input, completed night study to become an aerospace engineer in 1958, researched boundary-layer skin friction, and later worked in Equal Opportunity.
- The NACA pattern fits a broader history: work was feminized and poorly paid while framed as routine execution, then increasingly masculinized as software and computing acquired prestige and compensation.

## `Hidden Figures Collective.md` — people inside a collective system

- Aeronautical knowledge emerged from an interdependent network, not isolated “great individuals.” Wind tunnels, mechanics, engineers, computers, supervisors, and peer-checking practices made one another effective.
- Katherine Johnson calculated trajectories, launch windows, and emergency star-navigation charts for Mercury, Apollo, and Shuttle work. John Glenn requested her verification of electronic orbital calculations. She pushed into editorial meetings and co-authored 26 papers.
- Vera Huckel reduced sonic-boom data for supersonic aircraft, Apollo/Saturn V re-entry, and the SR-71; wrote an early custom Langley program; published extensively; and opened electronic-computing resources across divisions.
- Dorothy Vaughan combined supervision, FORTRAN knowledge, and peer training to move West Area staff toward programming work.
- Mary Jackson combined tunnel practice, boundary-layer research, publication, advanced study, engineering work, and later institutional advocacy.
- Helen Willey led an 8-Foot Transonic Tunnel pool for decades, produced a data-reduction handbook, and advocated for professional classification.
- Emma Jean Landrum invented a tape-reading gauge, earned the first physics master's awarded to a woman by William & Mary, and advanced to engineer.
- Collective quality control included independent recalculation and red-dot correction. Experienced computers trained newcomers and later wrote manuals and taught FORTRAN to operators and engineers.
- Social infrastructure mattered: dormitory life, carpools, recreational clubs, mentorship, and long friendships helped women sustain wartime shifts and precarious living arrangements.
- Long-term tunnel assignments produced expertise that allowed computers to challenge anomalies. Mary Jackson's dispute over faulty input illustrates that calculation involved judgment, not passive transcription.
- Career mobility was uneven and often required advocacy. Kazimir Czarnecki supported Jackson's publication and study; urgent data could move directly from leaders such as John Stack to the pools.

## `Pioneers of Code.md` — transition to electronic systems

- Electronic computing was a gradual, interdependent evolution, not an abrupt displacement. No ready-made aerodynamic software arrived with the mainframes.
- Machines could calculate quickly but did not understand pressure data, skin friction, lift, drag, or which outputs were physically plausible. Human computers translated scientific problems into wiring, punch cards, symbolic code, and checking routines.
- Vera Huckel wrote the first custom program for Langley's initial electronic computer and later promoted an “open shop” model for shared computing resources.
- Dorothy Vaughan learned FORTRAN and trained West Area colleagues and male engineers for flight and missile work, including Scout.
- Vivian Adair moved from hand calculation to plugboard wiring and electronic Wang calculators. Barbara Weigel entered the Electronic Computing Section and treated programs as puzzles that should be fast, self-checking, and error-detecting.
- Mary Jackson and Kathryn Peddrew contributed operating/training manuals, keypunch-card management, and selection of routines based on expected aerodynamic behavior.
- Electronic output still required trust. John Glenn asked Katherine Johnson to verify the Friendship 7 orbital figures; her manual baselines and star-navigation work offered backups for later missions.
- NASA's later decentralized “core systems” resembled the earlier arrangement of computing pools attached to specific research divisions and tunnels.
- Automation changed the visible artifact—from a room of women to a machine cabinet—while preserving hidden human work in programming, operation, checking, and instruction.

## `Hidden Computers (1).md` — evidence, memory, and missing voices

- Institutional archiving, social norms, and public memory nearly erased human computation from the official story.
- Beverly Golemba combined interviews, institutional documents, photographs, records, personal recollection, and peer networks. Destroyed personnel files prevented a complete roster.
- Golemba located 13 women and four male project engineers from a workforce numbering in the hundreds. Many others had died, moved, or could not be traced.
- Routine sheets and tapes were discarded after reports were complete. Helen Willey's preserved manometer tape is exceptional physical evidence of an otherwise ephemeral practice.
- Institutional history and recollection do not align neatly. Interviewees emphasized pride, patriotism, teamwork, and positive relations, while pay classifications, segregation, trivializing publications, and unequal credit document structural inequity.
- Public memory assumed female labor was temporary or ornamental. Comparable erasure affected ENIAC programmers, who were later mistaken for models in publicity photographs, and other wartime calculating pools whose members were omitted from final reports.
- Postwar family expectations, return to teaching, and attrition into retirement allowed names, techniques, and careers to disappear without formal recognition.
- What remains missing includes the anonymous majority, the everyday experience of many Black support workers, and tacit craft knowledge—mental shortcuts, calculator techniques, and informal procedures that were never written down.

## Cross-document synthesis

The most defensible overarching claim is that a “human computer” was simultaneously a person, an occupational classification, a quality-control practice, and a component in a larger flight-research system. The epistemic map should therefore reveal five linked structures:

1. a material workflow that turns airflow into numbers and numbers into design;
2. a social division of labor that made the workflow scalable;
3. a hierarchy that distributed pay, titles, credit, and opportunity unequally;
4. a continuity from manual calculation to software, with women carrying domain knowledge across the transition; and
5. an archival filter that determines whose work remains visible.
